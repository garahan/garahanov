People often ask me why I switched from Applied Chemistry to Policy Science. The honest answer is: I didn't switch. I expanded.

## The Engineer's Mindset

Four years of engineering at Tokai University taught me to think in systems. Break a problem into components. Model each component. Understand the interactions. Optimize. This way of thinking is permanent — it shapes how I approach everything, from research questions to team management at Apple.

Engineering also taught me humility: your model is always wrong, but some models are useful. The question isn't "is this perfect?" but "does this help me make a better decision than I would without it?"

## The Social Scientist's Toolkit

When I arrived at Sophia University as a research student in 2022, I felt like I'd landed on a different planet. Social scientists didn't talk about optimization or efficiency — they talked about institutions, incentives, and human behavior. They were skeptical of models that claimed to predict human systems.

That skepticism was valuable. It taught me that data without context is just numbers. A regression coefficient tells you *what* happened, but understanding *why* requires institutional knowledge, historical context, and qualitative understanding.

## The Intersection

At Waseda, I found the intersection: policy science that uses rigorous quantitative methods but remains grounded in real-world institutional understanding. This is where I can bring my engineering toolkit — modeling, optimization, systems thinking — to questions that actually matter to people's lives.

Some examples of questions that live at this intersection:

- How do energy policies affect economic outcomes in resource-dependent regions?
- Can we predict the impact of educational interventions using large-scale administrative data?
- What econometric methods best identify causal effects in observational policy data?

## The Apple Japan Dimension

Working at Apple Japan adds a third dimension. In a corporate environment, the questions are more immediate: how do we improve team performance? How do we communicate across cultures? But the method is the same — identify the problem from data, build a process, measure the outcome.

The difference is speed. In academia, a paper takes months. At Apple, a process improvement can be implemented in days. Both have value: academia for depth, industry for iteration.

## The Challenge

The hardest part of being interdisciplinary is that you never fully belong to either world. Engineers think you've gone soft. Social scientists think you're too quantitative. But I've come to see this as a feature, not a bug. The most interesting problems don't respect disciplinary boundaries.

## Advice for Anyone Considering a Similar Path

1. **Don't abandon your first field** — your engineering training is an asset, not a sunk cost
2. **Learn the language of your new field** — every discipline has its vocabulary and norms
3. **Find problems, not methods** — let the question drive the methodology, not the other way around
4. **Embrace the discomfort** — feeling like an outsider means you're seeing things others miss

The intersection of engineering and social science is not a compromise. It's a vantage point.
